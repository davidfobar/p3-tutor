Lesson 1 · 30 minutes

Endpoint velocity from joint rates: the Jacobian, one column per joint

By the end of this lesson you can write the Jacobian of a serial arm column by column, from its joint axes and joint positions, and use it to turn joint speeds into hand velocity.

Objective O2: Find the endpoint velocity from the joint rates About 30 minutes Builds on your forward kinematics (objective O1)
Why this matters for you

You want a recipe you can trust on an exam and in code, one that does not depend on a worked example for that exact robot. The recipe here needs only the things you can already compute: each joint's axis z and origin O in base coordinates. It is also the matrix that velocity control, force-to-torque mapping and inverse kinematics are built on.

Warm-up 2 min

This lesson uses what you showed in your first session: you can pull the joint axes z and frame origins O out of the chain of transforms. Try it once, from memory.

Recall

A planar two-link arm has link lengths a1 = 2 and a2 = 1, both joints rotary, axes parallel. At θ1 = 90° and θ2 = −90°, write O1 and O2 in base coordinates. Which axis does joint 2 turn about, and where is that axis located?

Check your answer

O1 = (0, 2, 0): link 1 points straight up. Link 2 points along the total angle θ1 + θ2 = 0°, so it points along +x, and O2 = (1, 2, 0). Joint 2 turns about z1, which is the base-frame vector (0, 0, 1), and that axis passes through O1. Joint i uses zi−1 and Oi−1, and that off-by-one is the most common slip in what follows.

The problem forward kinematics cannot solve 4 min

Picture a robot arm holding a pen, drawing a straight line on a whiteboard at a steady 0.2 m/s. The camera on the hand needs a constant speed, because a jerky line smears the image. Forward kinematics tells you where the pen is for a given set of joint angles. It does not tell you what the joints must do, at each instant, to make the pen move that way.

The motors do not take position commands along a line. They take speeds. So you need a map between two kinds of velocity:

  • Joint rates q̇ = (q̇1, …, q̇n): how fast each joint turns (rad/s) or slides (m/s).
  • Endpoint velocity V = (v, ω): the linear velocity v of a chosen point on the hand (m/s) and the angular velocity ω of the hand (rad/s), stacked into one six-number vector.

The Jacobian J(q) is the matrix that connects them:

V = J(q) q̇

It depends on the current joint angles q, so you recompute it as the arm moves. At any one instant the relation is linear, which is why a matrix works. The textbook treatments in Clemson's open robotics text and in Poonawala's notes on Spong's book both build the same object.

Can't I just differentiate the forward kinematics?

Partly. Your position equations give the hand's position p(q). By the chain rule, ṗ = (∂p/∂q) q̇, so the top three rows of J are exactly the partial derivatives of the position. That works, but the algebra explodes for a six-joint arm, and it gives you nothing for the bottom three rows. Angular velocity ω is not the derivative of an orientation such as roll-pitch-yaw. Differentiate those angles and you get a different matrix, the analytical Jacobian, which is not the one we want here (the notes treat the two separately).

The geometric method below skips the differentiation. It builds the matrix one joint at a time, from a picture you can check by eye.

Check yourself

You differentiate the three position equations of a six-joint arm with respect to the six joint angles. Which part of the Jacobian have you found?

Show the answer

Only the three linear-velocity rows. Derivatives of the position give the top block; the angular block comes from the joint axes.

What a column means 6 min

Write the Jacobian as columns: J = [ J1   J2   …   Jn ]. Multiplying out, V = J1 q̇1 + J2 q̇2 + … + Jn q̇n.

Read that as a sentence: the hand velocity is the sum of what each joint does on its own. Column i is the hand's six-number velocity when joint i moves at one unit per second (1 rad/s or 1 m/s) and every other joint is frozen. This works because the equation is linear at each instant, so effects add. It is also the picture behind your sanity check: freeze everything but one joint, and watch where the hand goes.

Here is a planar two-link arm with both links 1 m long, at θ1 = 0° and θ2 = 90°. The elbow O1 is at (1, 0) and the tip O2 at (1, 1).

Turning joint 1 alone swings the whole arm about the base. The tip moves on a circle of radius √2 around the origin, so its speed is √2 m/s, perpendicular to the line from the base to the tip. That is (−1, 1). Turning joint 2 alone swings only the forearm about the elbow, with radius 1, giving (−1, 0). Those two vectors are the two columns. Notice that you can read each one straight off the picture, and nothing here needed a derivative.

A common mix-up

"Column i depends only on joint i." It is tempting because column i is about joint i. But look at where the vectors come from: the direction of the joint's axis depends on all the joints before it, and the lever arm out to the tip depends on all the joints after it. In Figure 1, J2 points along −x because θ1 = 0. If joint 1 turned the arm up by 90°, J2 would change even though θ2 did not. Every column depends on the whole configuration.

The rotary column 6 min

A rotary joint i turns the whole rest of the arm rigidly about its axis. You already know two facts that give the column.

  • Linear part. A point at position r from the axis, on a body turning at rate ω, has velocity ω × r. Here the rate is 1 rad/s about the unit axis zi−1, and the point is the endpoint, so the linear part is zi−1 × (On − Oi−1).
  • Angular part. The whole hand turns at 1 rad/s about that axis, so the angular part is zi−1. (Angular velocities of bodies in series add as vectors, which is what lets the columns add.)

Stacked, this is the formula given in the Clemson text and in Spong, Hutchinson and Vidyasagar:

The recipe, rotary joint i

Ji = [ zi−1 × (On − Oi−1)  ;  zi−1 ]

  1. Get zi−1 and Oi−1 in base coordinates from 0Ti−1: the third column of its rotation block, and its position column.
  2. Get the endpoint On from 0Tn.
  3. Form the lever arm r = On − Oi−1: from the axis all the way to the endpoint.
  4. Cross in this order: axis first, lever arm second. Linear part on top, angular part (the axis itself) below.

Three details decide whether the recipe works. Each one matches a trap you may meet, so each is worth a sentence.

Three traps

Wrong lever arm. The vector along link i feels natural, because it is the link you can see. But joint i moves everything beyond it, so the lever arm runs to the endpoint. In Figure 1, using link 2's vector (0, 1) for joint 1 would give (−1, 0) instead of (−1, 1). It is right for the last joint, which is why a two-link example can hide the mistake.

Wrong frame. The axis is (0, 0, 1) in its own frame, always. The cross product needs the axis in the same frame as On − Oi−1, which is the base frame. Take it from 0Ti−1, not from the local frame.

Wrong order. r × z is the negative of z × r, so the hand moves the wrong way around. Check with the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand about the axis, and the tip moves in the direction your fingers go at the tip.

Check yourself

Two links of 1 m. At θ1 = 90° and θ2 = 0° the arm points straight up: O1 = (0, 1) and the tip is at (0, 2). Joint 2 turns at 1 rad/s and joint 1 is frozen. What is the tip velocity?

Show the answer

(−1, 0) m/s. The lever arm is O2 − O1 = (0, 1, 0), and z × (0, 1, 0) = (−1, 0, 0).

The endpoint is a choice

"The endpoint" is whichever point you name. Move it from the wrist to a tool tip 0.3 m further out, and every lever arm changes, so the linear rows change. The angular rows do not, because a rigid hand turns at one rate wherever you measure it. The tool tip's velocity is the wrist's plus ω × (the offset), which is the same rigid-body rule you already know.

The prismatic column 3 min

A prismatic joint slides everything beyond it along its axis, with no turning. Every point of the hand moves with the same velocity, the axis direction, and the hand's angular velocity does not change. The lever arm plays no role, because sliding is not a rotation about anything.

The recipe, prismatic joint i

Ji = [ zi−1  ;  0 ]

Same axis, taken in base coordinates. No cross product, and zero in the angular part.

Side by side, the rotary column is [ z × r ; z ] and the prismatic one is [ z ; 0 ]. In code, the only difference is one branch on the joint type.

Check yourself

A six-joint arm has a prismatic joint 3 with its axis along base +x. It extends at 0.4 m/s while everything else is frozen. What does the hand do?

Show the answer

The hand translates at 0.4 m/s along x and does not turn: column 3 is (1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0).

Worked example and practice 6 min

Worked example: the pen on the whiteboard

The two-link arm of Figure 1 (both links 1 m, θ1 = 0°, θ2 = 90°). The pen must move in +x at 0.2 m/s. Find the joint rates.

  1. Collect the ingredients in base coordinates. z0 = z1 = (0, 0, 1). O0 = (0, 0, 0), O1 = (1, 0, 0), O2 = (1, 1, 0).
  2. Column 1 (rotary): r = O2 − O0 = (1, 1, 0). z × r = (0·0 − 1·1, 1·1 − 0·0, 0) = (−1, 1, 0). Angular part: (0, 0, 1).
  3. Column 2 (rotary): r = O2 − O1 = (0, 1, 0). z × r = (−1, 0, 0). Angular part: (0, 0, 1).
  4. Sanity check against Figure 1. Both columns match the arrows we read off the picture. Good.
  5. Keep the rows you need. The pen moves in the plane and we do not care about its orientation, so use the x and y rows: J = [[−1, −1], [1, 0]]. Its determinant is (−1)(0) − (−1)(1) = 1, so it is invertible.
  6. Invert. J−1 = [[0, 1], [−1, −1]]. Then q̇ = J−1 (0.2, 0) = (0, −0.2).
  7. Check by intuition. Joint 1 stays still. Joint 2 turns clockwise at 0.2 rad/s, so the forearm, which points up from the elbow, swings to the right at 0.2 m/s. That is +x. ✓

Notice what the example shows. The matrix does the hard part, and no calculus was needed. The arm that draws the line is changing its Jacobian as it moves, so a real controller repeats steps 1 to 6 many times a second. This is resolved-rate control, one of the topics this lesson sets up.

Your turn: a rotary arm

The same arm (both links 1 m) is now at θ1 = 90° and θ2 = −90°. First step given: O1 = (0, 1, 0) and O2 = (1, 1, 0). Find the tip velocity when q̇1 = 1 rad/s and q̇2 = 2 rad/s, and say what the hand's angular velocity is.

Show a solution

Column 1: r = O2 − O0 = (1, 1, 0), z × r = (−1, 1, 0). Column 2: r = O2 − O1 = (1, 0, 0), z × r = (0, 1, 0). Sum: 1·(−1, 1) + 2·(0, 1) = (−1, 3) m/s. Angular velocity: 1·(0,0,1) + 2·(0,0,1) = (0, 0, 3) rad/s. Sanity check: turning joint 2 alone swings a forearm that points in +x, so the tip should move in +y. Column 2 = (0, 1). ✓

Worked example: a rotary joint and a prismatic joint

An arm with joint 1 rotary about z (through the origin) and joint 2 prismatic, sliding along the link. At θ1 = 90° the link points up, and the slide is extended to d = 2 m. The tip is at O2 = (0, 2, 0). Find the hand velocity for q̇1 = 0.5 rad/s and q̇2 = 0.3 m/s.

  1. Axes in base coordinates. z0 = (0, 0, 1). The slide axis z1 points along the link, which is up: z1 = (0, 1, 0).
  2. Column 1 (rotary): r = (0, 2, 0), z0 × r = (−2, 0, 0). Column: [(−2, 0, 0) ; (0, 0, 1)].
  3. Column 2 (prismatic): [z1 ; 0] = [(0, 1, 0) ; (0, 0, 0)].
  4. Combine. v = 0.5(−2, 0, 0) + 0.3(0, 1, 0) = (−1, 0.3, 0) m/s. ω = 0.5(0, 0, 1) = (0, 0, 0.5) rad/s.
  5. Check. Swinging the arm counter-clockwise from "up" moves the tip left (−x), and sliding out moves it up (+y). Both signs agree.

In code, and checked numerically

The recipe is a loop. Because the top rows are derivatives of the position, you can check them against finite differences of your forward kinematics. (The bottom rows cannot be checked this way; use the single-joint test in the next section.)

import numpy as np

def jacobian(axes, origins, p_end, kinds):
    """axes[i], origins[i]: base-frame z and O of joint i+1.
    kinds[i] is 'R' or 'P'. Returns the 6 x n matrix."""
    cols = []
    for z, O, k in zip(axes, origins, kinds):
        if k == 'R':
            cols.append(np.concatenate([np.cross(z, p_end - O), z]))
        else:
            cols.append(np.concatenate([z, np.zeros(3)]))
    return np.array(cols).T

def fk_2r(q, a1=1.0, a2=1.0):
    O1 = np.array([a1*np.cos(q[0]), a1*np.sin(q[0]), 0.0])
    O2 = O1 + np.array([a2*np.cos(q[0]+q[1]), a2*np.sin(q[0]+q[1]), 0.0])
    return O1, O2

def J_2r(q):
    O1, O2 = fk_2r(q)
    z = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0])
    return jacobian([z, z], [np.zeros(3), O1], O2, ['R', 'R'])

q, h = np.array([0.0, np.pi/2]), 1e-6
numeric = np.column_stack([(fk_2r(q + h*e)[1] - fk_2r(q - h*e)[1]) / (2*h)
                           for e in np.eye(2)])
print(np.allclose(J_2r(q)[:3], numeric, atol=1e-6))   # expect True

Run it at a few random q. A sign error or a wrong lever arm shows up at once as False.

Is my answer right? 3 min

Run these checks every time, in this order. Each takes under a minute.

  1. Units. A rotary column's linear part is metres per radian; a prismatic one is dimensionless (m per m). Angular rows are rad/rad or zero.
  2. One joint at a time. Move one joint in your head, or on a sketch. Does the hand go where that column says, including the sign?
  3. Perpendicularity. A rotary column's linear part must be perpendicular to both the axis and the lever arm. If it is not, you used the wrong vectors.
  4. Magnitude. The linear part has length |r⊥|, the distance from the endpoint to the axis. In Figure 1, the lengths were √2 and 1.
  5. Numerically, as in the code above.

When the matrix cannot be inverted

For the two-link arm, stretch it out: θ1 = 0°, θ2 = 0°. Then O2 = (2, 0), column 1 = z × (2, 0) = (0, 2) and column 2 = z × (1, 0) = (0, 1). The columns are parallel, the determinant is zero, and whatever the joints do, the tip can only move in y. The arm cannot extend its tip along its own length at that instant, and the inverse needs enormous joint rates when it is nearly stretched. This is a singularity: a configuration where the columns of J lose independence. You can now spot it from the picture, because the lever arms and axes line up. Later lessons build on it.

Summary 2 min

  • V = J(q) q̇. Column i is the hand's velocity when joint i moves at one unit per second and the others are frozen; the total is the sum.
  • Rotary joint i: [ zi−1 × (On − Oi−1) ; zi−1 ]. Prismatic: [ zi−1 ; 0 ].
  • Everything is in base coordinates, the lever arm runs to the endpoint, and the cross product is axis first.
  • Every column depends on the whole configuration, and moving the endpoint changes only the linear rows.
  • Angular velocity is not the derivative of an orientation, so do not differentiate roll-pitch-yaw for this matrix.
