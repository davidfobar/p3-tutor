From joint rates to hand speed: building the Jacobian column by column

By the end of this lesson you can build the Jacobian of a serial arm from its frames, multiply it by the joint rates to get the hand's linear and angular velocity, and check one column by moving one joint.

Why this matters for you

You want to write the Jacobian for an arm nobody has solved for you, answer "how fast is the hand moving?", and check your code against finite differences. This lesson gives you the recipe for the first two and the check for the third. Velocity control, force-torque mapping and inverse kinematics are all built on this one matrix.

A straight line at constant speed O2 2.5 min

Picture a six-joint arm sealing the seam along a car door. The sealant only lays down evenly if the nozzle moves along the seam at a steady speed, say 0.2 m/s. Forward kinematics, which you already have, answers a different question: given the joint angles, where is the nozzle? It says nothing about how fast to turn each joint so that the nozzle glides along a line. Each joint has to turn at a different, changing rate, and you need a way to compute those rates.

The tool is one matrix. The Jacobian J, which depends on the current joint angles, turns the vector of joint rates q̇ into the hand's velocity V, a stack of its linear velocity v and its angular velocity ω:

V = [v; ω] = J(q) · q̇

This is the matrix that links joint rates to the hand's linear and angular velocity (Stanford CS223A, Lecture 7). This lesson is the forward direction: given the rates, find the hand velocity. Going the other way, from a wanted hand velocity to joint rates, means inverting J, and that comes in a later lesson.

Read the matrix by its shape. For an arm with n joints, J has six rows and n columns: the top three rows give how fast the hand moves through space, the bottom three give how fast it turns. Column i answers one question only: "if joint i alone turned at one unit per second, what would the hand do?" Row by row it is hard to picture, but column by column you can sketch it on paper, and that is how the rest of this lesson works. A six-joint arm has six such sketches to make, one per joint.

Why not just differentiate your forward-kinematics equations? For the position rows you can, and the answer matches. The trouble is the orientation rows, which section 3 covers. The geometric recipe below also never needs the full symbolic pose, only the frames you already know how to compute.

Warm-up O1 2.5 min

The whole recipe rests on two things you can already do: reading axes and origins out of the intermediate transforms. Try one from memory before you look.

Recall

A planar arm has rotary joints whose axes are parallel to the base z axis. Link 1 has length a₁ = 0.6 m and joint 1 is at θ₁ = 30°. Write ⁰z₀ and ⁰O₁ in base coordinates. Then say which part of which transform each one comes from.

Check your answer

⁰z₀ = (0, 0, 1), and ⁰O₁ = (0.6 cos 30°, 0.6 sin 30°, 0) = (0.520, 0.300, 0) m. The axis is the third column of the rotation part of ⁰T₀ (the identity, so (0, 0, 1)). The origin is the position part of ⁰T₁. Today you use exactly these two pieces for every joint.

Recall, part two

Continue the same arm with a₂ = 0.4 m and θ₂ = 60°, so the second link points 90° from the x axis. Where is the hand, O₂, in base coordinates, and which transform's position part gives it? Why do you need O₂ − O₁ rather than O₂ alone when you build column 2 later?

Check your answer

O₂ = O₁ + 0.4·(cos 90°, sin 90°) = (0.520, 0.700, 0) m, read from the position part of ⁰T₂. Column 2 spins the hand about joint 2, not about the base, so the arm is measured from joint 2's origin, and O₂ − O₁ = (0, 0.4, 0) is that arm. The same numbers return in the worked example.

A common mix-up

"Every joint axis is (0, 0, 1)." In its own frame, yes: each joint turns about its frame's z axis, so it looks like (0, 0, 1). But the cross product in the recipe mixes it with a vector in base coordinates, so the axis must be rewritten in the base frame first. In this planar arm the parallel axes happen to stay (0, 0, 1), which hides the mistake. In a spatial arm, joint 4's axis is typically tilted in the base frame, and the shortcut gives a wrong answer.

One joint at a time O2 5.5 min

Here is the idea that makes the matrix easy to build. Freeze every joint except joint i and turn it at one radian per second. Everything beyond joint i now moves as a single rigid body spinning about joint i's axis, and you already know the velocity of a point on a spinning body: v = ω × r. Here ω is the axis direction times the rate, and r is the vector from a point on the axis to the point you care about (Lecture 7). The hand's motion from that one joint is column i of J.

Think of a car's steering linkage. Turn only the steering wheel and the wheel hub swings around the kingpin; it moves perpendicular to the line from the kingpin to the hub, and farther hubs move faster. A robot joint is the same: one pivot, one rigid arm out to the point of interest, motion at right angles to that arm.

Because angular velocities of bodies in series add, and the hand's linear velocity from each joint adds too, the total is the sum of the columns, each scaled by its joint's rate: V = q̇₁·(column 1) + q̇₂·(column 2) + …. That sum is the matrix-vector product J·q̇.

Try one column by hand before the demo. A planar arm, joint 1 at the origin, hand at (0.5, 0.8) m, joint 1 turning at +2 rad/s with joint 2 held still. The axis is (0, 0, 1) and the arm to the hand is (0.5, 0.8, 0), so the hand moves at 2 · (0, 0, 1) × (0.5, 0.8, 0) = 2 · (−0.8, 0.5, 0) = (−1.6, 1.0, 0) m/s. Check it against the picture: the hand is up and to the right of the pivot, and counterclockwise rotation swings it up and to the left. The arrow is also at right angles to the arm, since (−0.8, 0.5) · (0.5, 0.8) = 0. That perpendicularity is your quickest sanity test on any column.

A common mix-up

"Each row of J is the derivative of one pose coordinate, so I differentiate the pose." The top three rows really are: differentiating the hand's position with respect to the joints gives the linear part. The bottom three are not. Angular velocity is not the derivative of roll-pitch-yaw angles, and differentiating those gives a different matrix. The geometric columns avoid the problem because they build angular velocity directly from the joint axes, where angular velocities of bodies in series add.

Check yourself

In Figure 1's arm, only joint 2 turns, at +1 rad/s. The hand's velocity is perpendicular to which line?

Show the answer

The line from joint 2 to the hand. The arm for column i always runs from joint i's axis to the endpoint.

The recipe: rotary and prismatic side by side O2 4 min

Put the one-joint idea in symbols. Joint i acts about or along the axis zi−1, and the hand is at origin On. Both vectors are written in base coordinates. A joint's column has a linear part on top and an angular part below it, and it depends on the joint's type (Lecture 7):

Joint typeLinear part (top)Angular part (bottom)
Rotaryzi−1 × (On − Oi−1)zi−1
Prismaticzi−10

A prismatic joint slides the whole rest of the arm along its axis. Every point translates at the rate of the slide, and nothing rotates, which is why the linear part is just the axis and the angular part is zero. Think of a car-wash gantry carriage rolling along its rail: the brush moves at carriage speed no matter how far out it hangs.

The procedure, with each step once per joint:

  1. Compute ⁰Ti for every joint, from the DH frames you already know how to build.
  2. Read the axis zi−1 as the third column of the rotation part of ⁰Ti−1, in base coordinates.
  3. Read the origins Oi−1 and On from the position parts of ⁰Ti−1 and ⁰Tn.
  4. Rotary joint: cross the axis with the arm, axis first, zi−1 × (On − Oi−1), and stack zi−1 below it. Prismatic joint: zi−1 on top, zeros below.
  5. Put the columns side by side to get the 6 × n matrix J, then compute V = J q̇.

Here is where the base-frame rule earns its keep. Take a shoulder-and-elbow arm: joint 1 turns about the vertical, and joint 2's axis is horizontal. In its own frame, joint 2's axis is (0, 0, 1). With a skew angle α₁ = 90° between the two axes, the third column of the rotation part of ⁰T₁ gives the axis in base coordinates as z₁ = (sin θ₁, −cos θ₁, 0). It is horizontal and it swings around as joint 1 turns, which is exactly why column 2 depends on θ₁. Crossing the local (0, 0, 1) with a base-frame arm would give a vector with the wrong direction, and nothing in the arithmetic would warn you. The sign here flips if you point x₁ the other way, as the course allows, so read it from your own transform rather than copying it.

Order matters: z × r points the way a counterclockwise spin pushes the point, and r × z points exactly the opposite. If your first column points the wrong way, swap the order, then confirm with the sketch check in the next sections.
Two mix-ups that hide on a two-link arm

Wrong arm: using the vector along link i instead of the vector from joint i's axis to the endpoint. For the last joint the two are the same, so checking only the last column passes; checking column 1 fails. The demo's red dashed arrow showed this. One column, one joint: column i depends on θi alone. No: it depends on earlier joints through zi−1 and Oi−1, and on later joints through On. Only a joint's own angle drops out of its own axis.

A three-joint arm: joint 1 rotary, joint 2 prismatic, joint 3 rotary. Which statement about column 2 is right?

Show the answer

Top part z₁, bottom part zero. The axis is joint 2's, written z₁ in the standard DH numbering.

Reference point, zero blocks, trouble spots O2 3 min

"The endpoint" is a choice. Take the wrist center, or the tool tip a few centimeters beyond it: both are fine, but they are different points on the same rigid body, so they move differently when the body spins. Switching the reference point changes only the linear rows, because a rigid body has one angular velocity no matter which point you look at. In the recipe, swap On for the new point and nothing else.

The same formula explains zero blocks. If a joint's axis passes through the reference point, the arm from that axis to the point is zero or parallel to the axis, so the cross product vanishes and the column has no linear part. A wrist whose three axes meet at one point produces exactly this when the reference point is that meeting point: turning a wrist joint does not move it, only the tool beyond it swings (Lecture 7). The last three columns then have zero linear rows and the axes in their angular rows, and the 6 × 6 matrix splits into blocks. Whenever you see a column with a zero top half, ask which axis passes through the point.

Finally, the geometry shows when the arm gets into trouble. Stretch a two-link arm straight out, as in Figure 2. Both arms, from joint 1 and from joint 2, now lie along one line, so both columns are perpendicular to that line and point the same way. No combination of q̇₁ and q̇₂ moves the hand along the arm. That is a singularity: the columns have become dependent, and a whole direction of hand motion is lost (Lecture 7). Near one, a small hand motion in the lost direction would need enormous joint rates, which is where the next lessons pick up.

Check yourself

You move the reference point from the wrist center to a tool tip 0.1 m further out along the last link. Which part of J changes?

Show the answer

Only the linear rows, because only the arms On − Oi−1 change.

Worked example and practice O2 4.5 min

Worked example: planar two-link arm

Both joints are rotary about base z. Link lengths a₁ = 0.6 m and a₂ = 0.4 m. The pose is θ₁ = 30°, θ₂ = 60°, and the rates are q̇₁ = 0.5 rad/s and q̇₂ = −1 rad/s. Find the hand's linear and angular velocity.

  1. Axes. Both axes are z₀ = z₁ = (0, 0, 1) in the base frame, as the warm-up showed.
  2. Origins. O₀ = (0, 0, 0). O₁ = 0.6·(cos 30°, sin 30°) = (0.520, 0.300, 0). The total angle is θ₁ + θ₂ = 90°, so the hand is O₂ = O₁ + 0.4·(cos 90°, sin 90°) = (0.520, 0.700, 0).
  3. Column 1: z₀ × (O₂ − O₀) = (0, 0, 1) × (0.520, 0.700, 0) = (−0.700, 0.520, 0). The arm is from joint 1 to the hand, not link 1. Angular part: (0, 0, 1).
  4. Column 2: z₁ × (O₂ − O₁) = (0, 0, 1) × (0, 0.400, 0) = (−0.400, 0, 0). Angular part: (0, 0, 1).
  5. Combine: v = 0.5·(−0.700, 0.520) + (−1)·(−0.400, 0) = (0.050, 0.260) m/s. ω = (0.5 − 1)·(0, 0, 1) = (0, 0, −0.5) rad/s.
  6. Check against the derivative of the position, x = 0.6 cos θ₁ + 0.4 cos(θ₁+θ₂), y = 0.6 sin θ₁ + 0.4 sin(θ₁+θ₂). Then ∂x/∂θ₁ = −0.6 sin 30° − 0.4 sin 90° = −0.700 and ∂y/∂θ₁ = 0.6 cos 30° + 0.4 cos 90° = 0.520. These match column 1 exactly. Also ∂x/∂θ₂ = −0.400 and ∂y/∂θ₂ = 0, matching column 2. Units: m × rad/s gives m/s. Direction: the hand points straight up from joint 2, so turning joint 2 counterclockwise swings it to the left, −x, as the sign shows.

That last step is the check you will code: compute a column analytically, then nudge one joint by a tiny angle, recompute forward kinematics, and divide the change in position by the nudge. The two should agree to several digits.

Notice what the worked example shows about the rates. The two joints turn in opposite directions, so their effects partly cancel in x (−0.350 from joint 1, +0.400 from joint 2) and add up to only 0.050 m/s. A joint-space speed that looks big can still give a slow hand. Equally, the angular velocity is a plain sum of the signed rates, −0.5 rad/s, because both axes point the same way. When axes point different ways, as in a spatial arm, that sum becomes a vector sum of rate-weighted axes, and the same bookkeeping holds.

As code, the whole check is short. This sketch assumes you have your own functions fk(q), returning the hand position, and jacobian(q), returning the 6 × n matrix:

def check_jacobian(q, eps=1e-6):
    J = jacobian(q)                      # analytic, 6 x n
    p0 = fk(q)                           # hand position (3,)
    for i in range(len(q)):
        dq = list(q)
        dq[i] += eps                     # nudge joint i only
        col_fd = (fk(dq) - p0) / eps     # finite-difference column
        assert max(abs(col_fd - J[:3, i])) < 1e-4, i

If column i fails, you know exactly which joint's axis or arm is wrong, so you debug one column rather than the whole matrix. For the bottom three rows, write the expected axis by hand for a pose you can sketch, and compare.

Your turn: a gantry with a swinging arm

A car-wash gantry carriage slides along the base x axis (joint 1, prismatic, axis z₀ = (1, 0, 0)). On the carriage, a brush arm of length 0.8 m swings about a vertical axis (joint 2, rotary, axis z₁ = (0, 0, 1)). At this moment the arm points along +y, so the brush is at O₂ = O₁ + (0, 0.8, 0). The carriage moves at ḋ₁ = 0.3 m/s and the arm swings at q̇₂ = 0.5 rad/s. Step 1 is given: column 1 is [z₀; 0] = (1, 0, 0; 0, 0, 0). Find column 2, then v and ω.

Show a solution

Column 2: linear = z₁ × (O₂ − O₁) = (0, 0, 1) × (0, 0.8, 0) = (−0.8, 0, 0); angular = z₁ = (0, 0, 1). Then v = 0.3·(1, 0, 0) + 0.5·(−0.8, 0, 0) = (0.3 − 0.4, 0, 0) = (−0.1, 0, 0) m/s, and ω = 0.3·0 + 0.5·(0, 0, 1) = (0, 0, 0.5) rad/s. Check: the slide alone moves the brush at +0.3 m/s in x; the swing alone moves it at 0.4 m/s in −x, because the arm along +y turns counterclockwise; the sum is −0.1 m/s. The prismatic joint added no angular part. If you got +0.4 for the swing, you crossed in the wrong order.

One more check on your own O2 1 min

Predict before you compute

Take the worked arm again, same pose, but now turn only joint 2 at +1 rad/s. Without computing, say which way the hand moves, then name the column that gives the number. After that, say what happens to the columns if θ₁ and θ₂ both become 0, so the arm is stretched along the x axis.

Show a solution

Joint 2 alone moves the hand by column 2, (−0.4, 0, 0) m/s, to the left. The second link points straight up from joint 2, and a counterclockwise spin pushes its tip left. With θ₁ = θ₂ = 0, the hand is at (1.0, 0, 0), so column 1 = z × (1.0, 0, 0) = (0, 1.0, 0) and column 2 = z × (0.4, 0, 0) = (0, 0.4, 0). Both point up the y axis, and that is the singularity of Figure 2.

Summary O2 1 min

  • V = J q̇: each column is the hand's motion from a unit rate at that joint alone, and the hand velocity is the sum of columns scaled by the rates.
  • Rotary column: [zi−1 × (On − Oi−1); zi−1]. Prismatic column: [zi−1; 0]. All in base coordinates, axis first in the cross product.
  • The arm runs from joint i's axis to the endpoint, never along link i.
  • Changing the reference point changes only the linear rows; axes through the point give zero linear columns; parallel columns mean a singularity.
  • Check by nudging one joint, and by sketching, units and direction.
