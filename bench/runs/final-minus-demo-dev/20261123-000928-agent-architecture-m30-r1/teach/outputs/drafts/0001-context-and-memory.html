What the assistant sees, and what it remembers

By the end of this lesson you can say what goes in the makerspace assistant's context, what it saves between sessions, and how it finds the right page of a manual.

Why the assistant must remember O3 2 min

Picture a mechanic who works with a short-term memory of exactly one page. Every few seconds the page is wiped, and before it is, someone has to hand them a new page that contains everything they need: the job, the tools on the bench, what they have done so far. A language model behaves like that mechanic, and your assistant's design is mostly the question of what to write on the page.

Before the details, one unit you will see everywhere. A token is the small chunk of text, often a short word or part of a word, that a model reads and writes; you met this in the lesson on what a language model does. Tokens matter for two reasons: the context window is measured in tokens, so it limits how much the model can look at in one call, and usage is generally priced per token, so more tokens in each call means a bigger bill. Both reasons come back below.

The technical word for the page is context: the set of tokens included when the model is sampled, that is, when it is asked to write its next output (Anthropic, Effective context engineering for AI agents). The limited space it fits in is the context window. Your assistant has three kinds of information to place, and this lesson gives each one a home:

  • What it needs right now (this student's question, the booking it is checking): in the window.
  • What it must know next week (a student's training status): in notes saved outside the window.
  • What is too big to carry (the laser cutter manual): in a store it searches and loads from on demand.

The context window is a budget O3 6 min

Think of the window as the cargo capacity of a truck. A truck can carry only so much, and even before it is full, a badly loaded truck handles worse. Everything the model reads in a call counts as cargo (Anthropic):

  • the instructions you wrote for the assistant (its job, its rules, its tone);
  • the tool descriptions, which the model needs in order to ask for the right action;
  • the history: the student's messages and the model's own earlier replies and requests;
  • the tool results: the calendar rows, the manual text, anything a tool returned.

Here is what each part looks like in the makerspace assistant, and how it behaves over a conversation. Notice that only two of the four are under your direct control when you design the system, and that the other two arrive during the conversation.

The practical lesson of the table: the two fixed parts are cheap to keep tidy, and the two growing parts are where your window gets crowded. A single page of manual can be longer than the entire conversation that led to it, so tool results are the first place to look when the page gets heavy.

Two costs follow from this, and both bite your project.

Cost one: quality. The window is limited, and it also degrades as it fills. The same source describes context rot: as the number of tokens in the window grows, the model's ability to accurately recall information from it decreases. It frames this as an "attention budget" that every new token draws down a little. The decline is gradual, not a cliff, so a window that still fits can already be working against you.

Cost two: money. Model usage is generally billed by the tokens you send and receive, and the whole page is re-sent on every call (Figure 1), so a long page is paid for again at every step of the loop. Some providers discount a repeated, unchanged start of the page through caching; check your provider's pricing page before you rely on that. As an illustration with made-up numbers, not real prices: if a page of 20,000 tokens goes out on each of 5 calls, you send 100,000 tokens for one request; trim the page to 5,000 and you send 25,000. Your sponsor's budget is a reason to keep the page short, not only your design taste.

A common mix-up

"The model remembers the earlier steps, so I only need to send the new message." It is tempting because a chat app feels like a continuous conversation. But the continuity comes from the program re-sending the whole history, as in Figure 1. If your code sends only the newest message, the model has no idea what the student asked a minute ago, because it only sees what is in the current context.

Within a session: what to keep between steps O3 4 min

Inside one conversation, the question is what to leave on the page at each step. Not everything that has happened deserves to stay. Compare it to a pit crew: the driver does not need the full history of the race read back every lap, only the current tire state and the plan.

There are two main tools, both described in the same Anthropic article:

  • Compaction: when the conversation nears the limit, summarize its contents and start a new window with that summary. The summary keeps the decisions and unresolved issues and drops the redundant detail.
  • Clearing old tool results: once a tool result has been used, its raw text rarely needs to stay. The article calls this one of the safest, lightest-touch forms of compaction.

For the makerspace assistant, the clearing case is natural. After it reads a calendar page of 40 rows and finds the free slot, it needs "Friday 2 pm is free", not the 40 rows.

When should the assistant compact rather than clear? A rough guide for your design, which is a judgment of mine and not a rule from the source:

  • Clear a tool result when you have already pulled out the one fact you needed from it.
  • Compact the history when the conversation itself has grown long, for instance a student who books three machines in one sitting and asks six side questions.
  • Keep in the summary: what the student asked for, what has been booked, what is still open. Drop the rest.

Think of it as the difference between emptying the glovebox of old receipts (clearing) and rewriting a long trip log into three lines before the next leg (compaction). Both free space; the second one risks losing a detail you wanted, so use it only when clearing is not enough.

Compaction is a judgment call: a summary that drops a detail the model needs later cannot be undone. Clearing tool results is gentler because the useful part was already used.
Check yourself

The assistant has just read a long laser cutter manual page to answer "what is the maximum material thickness?" and has given the answer. The student's next question is about booking. What is the lightest sensible step?

Show the answer

Clear the used page and keep the answer already given. The raw text served its purpose.

Between sessions: notes as memory O3 5 min

Everything so far lives in the window, and the window does not survive the end of the session. When the student closes the chat and comes back Thursday, the new page starts blank. Anything that must outlast the session has to be written somewhere outside the window and read back in.

This is note-taking memory: the agent regularly writes notes to storage outside the context window, and those notes are pulled back into the window at later times (Anthropic). The article's example is Claude playing Pokémon, keeping tallies and maps in notes across thousands of game steps. A service logbook in a car's glovebox is the same idea. The mechanic does not remember your Tacoma, but the logbook says the brake pads were replaced in June, so the next shop starts informed.

You have already seen this pattern from the other side: this workspace keeps a NOTES.md that the tutor reads at the start of every session.

For your assistant, a good note is small and specific. A procedure for what to save:

  • Decide what a later session truly needs, such as "completed laser cutter training on March 3; not yet trained on the CNC router".
  • Write it at the end of the session, as a tool call such as save_note(student, text) that the program runs.
  • At the start of the next session, the program reads the note and puts it into the window as part of the instructions.

Two cautions about notes, both about design rather than technology:

  • Save at a defined moment. If the assistant forgets to save a training result, nothing else will remember it, so saving should happen right after a training is confirmed, not "whenever the model feels like it".
  • Notes go stale. "Not yet trained on the CNC router" is wrong the day after the student passes, so whoever records training should update the note, or the assistant should check the authoritative record when the answer matters.

Keep notes short, dated and about the student, and let big documents stay in the retrieval store.

A common mix-up

"A model with a bigger window would remember students on its own." It is tempting because a large window sounds like a large memory. But a window is only the page for one session. It is empty at the start of the next one however large it was. Saved notes outlast the session; the window does not (Anthropic). A bigger window that you actually fill also makes context rot and per-call cost worse, not better.

Retrieval: finding the right manual page O3 4 min

The manuals are the third kind of information: far too big to put in the window and too useful to leave out. Pasting every manual into every call would cost money on each step (Figure 1) and invite context rot (Figure 2). The alternative is just-in-time retrieval. The agent keeps lightweight identifiers, such as file paths or stored queries, and uses tools to load data into context at runtime (Anthropic).

Think of the index at the back of a service manual. You do not read the whole manual to find the torque spec. You look up "wheel nuts", get a page number, and open that page. The identifier is the page number, and loading is turning to the page.

How does the model choose which page to load? It can only choose from what it can see. So the window carries a short, readable list: each manual's file name and a one-line description, kept with the tool description for load_page. Given the question "what speed for 3 mm acrylic on the laser cutter?", the model reads the list, sees that laser.md is the laser cutter manual and router.md is the CNC router manual, and asks for load_page("laser.md", "acrylic"). The same reasoning is why your file names and one-line descriptions matter: they are the only thing the model has to decide with. Vague names like manual2.md would send it to the wrong page, or to load several pages to be safe, which brings back the cost of a crowded window.

  • If the first page does not answer the question, the model can ask for another page; this is the loop again.
  • Load a section, not a whole manual, whenever the store lets you.
  • After the answer is given, the page can be cleared, as in the previous section.

This is the same agent loop you already know. The "action" is a tool that loads a page and the "result" is the page text, so retrieval is not a new mechanism, only a use of tool calling. How to cut a manual into pages and rank them for a question is a topic for a later lesson; the idea to hold today is that the window carries pointers and the store carries the text.

Worked example and practice O3 4 min

The skill here is sorting information into the right home. Use this rule of thumb from the three sections above:

  • Window: needed in this session, and small.
  • Notes: needed in a future session, and small.
  • Retrieve on demand: large, and needed only sometimes.
Worked example: one request, all three homes

A returning student, Sam, writes: "Can I book the laser cutter Friday at 2? Also what is the max thickness for acrylic?" Follow the page on each call.

  1. Start of session. The program loads Sam's note ("laser cutter training done March 3") and puts it with the instructions and tool descriptions. The window holds: rules, tool descriptions, Sam's note, Sam's question. The manuals are not in it, only their file paths.
  2. Call 1. The model asks for check_calendar. The program runs it and appends 40 rows.
  3. Call 2. The page is now rules, tools, note, question, request, 40 rows. The model sees Friday 2 pm is free and that Sam's note shows laser training, so it asks to book. The program books it. The 40 rows are now dead weight, so clear them and keep "Friday 2 pm booked".
  4. Call 3. The model asks to load laser.md, page "acrylic". The program appends that one page, not the manual.
  5. Call 4. The model answers both questions. The page can then drop the manual text.
  6. End of session. The assistant saves nothing new about Sam's training, because nothing changed. If Sam had finished a new course, this is when save_note would run.
Worked example: sorting items

Sort: (a) the sentence "I need the 3D printer on Friday afternoon", (b) a student's record of finishing the 3D printer safety course, (c) the 60-page 3D printer manual.

  1. (a) is needed now and is one line: it goes in the window. After the booking is made it can drop out.
  2. (b) is needed at the next visit and is tiny: it goes in notes, because the window will be gone by then.
  3. (c) is large and only one page is ever needed: retrieve on demand, with the file path as the pointer.

One more practice check O3 2 min

A teammate proposes: "Let us simply keep the full history of every student's every visit in the window, so the assistant never forgets anything." Use what you know to judge it.

Another proposal, from a different teammate: "Give the assistant one big note file with everything about every student, and load the whole file at the start of every session." The file holds 300 students at about 150 words each. Think about what a returning student needs: their own few lines. Loading all 300 records puts 299 irrelevant records in the window, with the same cost and rot problems, and it also mixes one student's information into another's conversation. A better design stores one note per student, keyed by an identifier such as their student ID, and loads only that one. This is the lightweight-identifier idea from the retrieval section applied to notes.

Check yourself

What is the strongest objection to the teammate's proposal?

Show the answer

The third option. It also cannot work across sessions, since the window starts blank each time; the right design saves a short note per student and loads only that.

  • Context is the page of tokens sent on every call; the model remembers nothing between calls except what the program re-sends.
  • The window is a limited budget: a crowded one hurts recall (context rot) and costs more on every step. Keep it short with compaction and by clearing used tool results.
  • Between sessions, only saved notes survive; a bigger window is not memory.
  • Big sources like manuals stay outside the window as pointers and are loaded just in time with a tool.
