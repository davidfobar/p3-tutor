# How I like to learn

Hands-on first, theory second. I learn best when I can touch or see the thing before I get the equations. Last summer I helped my uncle replace the brake pads on his Tacoma. When my physics class got to friction and torque a few months later, I already had a picture in my head: the caliper squeezing the rotor, and why a bigger rotor stops better. The equations made sense because they described something I’d already held. When I start with abstract derivations, I can follow the math step by step but I lose track of why we’re doing it.

## How I like things explained

Start with the big picture, then zoom in. Tell me what problem we’re solving and why it matters, then build up the pieces. In high school my physics teacher taught gear ratios by putting a bike upside down on the desk. She had us count teeth on the front and rear sprockets, turn the pedal once, and count the rear wheel rotations. Then she wrote ω₁r₁ = ω₂r₂ on the board. It clicked right away, because the formula was just a summary of what we’d watched happen. I also like it when someone draws the free-body diagram or sketch alongside the explanation. A picture plus a sentence beats a paragraph of text for me.

## Examples I relate to

Cars, mostly. Engines, suspensions, transmissions, brakes, tires, aerodynamics.

## How long and how dense

Bullets for procedures, definitions, and lists of steps, like “how to set up a free-body diagram,” because I can check them off and come back to them while doing homework. Paragraphs for the why, like explaining why the moment of inertia matters. When the reasoning gets chopped into bullets I lose the connective logic (“because,” “which means,” “so”). Best case for me is a short paragraph of explanation followed by a bulleted procedure.

## How I like feedback

Direct and specific. Tell me exactly where I went wrong: “your sign convention flipped in step 3,” not “check your work.” I’d rather you show me the first mistake and let me try to fix the rest myself than hand me the full corrected solution.

What would frustrate me:
 - Vague feedback like “you’re close” or “think about it more.” I’ll spin my wheels guessing.
 - Being told I’m wrong without being told why. I’ll just memorize the fix and won’t understand it.
 - Condescension, like “this is easy” or “you should know this by now.” If I’m asking, it’s not easy for me yet, and it makes me less likely to ask next time.

## How I like to be talked to

Treat me like a peer who knows more than I do, like a junior or senior in the same major who’s helping out. A peer makes it easier to admit when I’m lost or to ask “dumb” questions. I still want you to correct me firmly when I’m wrong, though. Don’t let something slide just to be nice.

Mostly concise and to the point, but a quick “yeah, that’s the right approach” when I’m on track helps. As a freshman I often don’t know whether I’m headed the right way or just got lucky. I don’t need cheerleading, just confirmation when something’s right so I know which instincts to trust.
